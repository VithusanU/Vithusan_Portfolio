## Overview

On a Vibe is a Toronto mobile coffee and cocktail cart booked for weddings,
corporate events, and parties. A host pays for the cart; their guests drink for
free.

The engagement ran May – August 2026 as a combined BA / product owner and build
role: requirements gathering, a written concept and PRD, a business case with a
prioritized investment order, and then the build. What shipped is a live
three-surface product — guest ordering, a bartender board, and an owner console.

[Live guest ordering →](https://on-a-vibe-cart.vercel.app) &nbsp;|&nbsp; [Printable QR sign →](https://on-a-vibe-cart.vercel.app/qr)

*Source is a private client repository. Staff screens are behind a login.*

## The Insight That Shaped Everything

The brief was "a QR code so people can order from their phone." Discovery
reframed it around a gap that turned out to drive nearly every subsequent
decision:

|  | Guest | Host |
|---|---|---|
| Pays | Never | Books and pays for the cart |
| Wants | A good drink, no awkward wait | Their event to feel considered |
| Uses | The QR page, for about four minutes | The recap, once, afterwards |

**The host is the customer. The guest is the experience being sold to them.**

That single distinction settled arguments that would otherwise have been matters
of taste. The clearest one: **no price appears anywhere in the guest app** — not
greyed out, not struck through, not in a total. A guest who sees a price starts
doing arithmetic about a gift, which is the opposite of the feeling the host paid
for. It also means the app has no checkout, no cart total, and no payment
surface at all.

## What the Product Is Actually For

Written down as three jobs, in priority order, so that scope arguments had
something to resolve against:

**1. Give people something to talk about.** The cart sits in a room where people
are standing around; the drinks are the excuse to start a conversation. Hence the
spinnable 3D drink, the per-drink colour and typeface, and the live "what
everyone's drinking" list. This is why the ordering screen is one big drink
rather than a menu list — *a list is something you read alone.*

**2. Get an undecided person out of the queue.** The slowest part of any drinks
queue isn't pouring; it's a person at the front who doesn't know what they want,
being watched by people who do. The app moves that decision onto their phone
while they're still walking over. For the genuinely stuck there's **Can't
decide** — two drinks, pick one, a few rounds, done. It had to feel like a game
and finish in seconds; if it ever felt like a form it would have failed, because
the stress it removes is the entire reason it exists.

![Can't decide — two drinks side by side, pick one, three rounds](/images/projects/on-a-vibe/guest-decide.png)

**3. Never slow the bartender down.** Everything on the bar screen is actionable,
sized to be read over a grinder at arm's length, with targets big enough to hit
with the side of a hand. Every forward tap is reversible — a mis-tap on "picked
up" used to drop a drink off the board with the guest still standing there. And
the app is never mandatory: the bartender can add a walk-up order in three taps,
so nobody is ever told to scan a QR code to get a coffee.

## Requirements With Real Consequences

A few requirements had teeth, and they're the ones worth showing:

**Allergens are legally weighted, so "not declared" and "no allergens" must never
collapse into each other.** An empty list is the owner stating there are none;
an absent field is nobody having answered yet. An undeclared drink tells guests
to ask at the bar rather than implying it's safe. The allergen sheet is also the
one screen that ignores the drink's theme and stays on a neutral card — it's read
to make a decision about someone's health, not to be charming.

![The allergen sheet — an undeclared drink says so rather than implying it is safe](/images/projects/on-a-vibe/guest-allergens.png)

**Nothing that identifies a guest may be publicly readable.** A Firebase project
id ships inside every client bundle, so anything a guest's phone can read is
effectively readable by the whole internet. Database rules can allow or deny a
whole document but cannot hide a field — so each order is written as two
documents in one atomic batch: a staff copy holding the name and note, readable
only by a signed-in staff account, and a public anonymous mirror carrying status,
time, drink and a pickup code. That's what lets a guest's phone show their place
in the queue without publishing the room's guest list.

**No keyboard between a guest and a drink.** The order sends immediately on a
press-and-hold gesture; the name is asked afterwards, while they wait, and can be
declined outright, because every order already carries a three-character pickup
code. The name is remembered on the device, so it's only ever asked once.

**An event day starts at 04:00, not midnight.** A party running to 1am used to
lose its queue and its numbers mid-service.

## Deliberate Omissions

Each was written down with the reason, so the decision stays revisitable by
someone else without me in the room:

- **No payments.** There's no checkout, tipping, or card reader. Adding one would
  change what the cart *is* at someone's wedding.
- **No guest accounts.** A QR code pointing at a login screen is a QR code nobody
  scans. A guest's order lives in their own browser storage.
- **No time estimates.** Queue position only ever counts down. "About 2 minutes"
  can be contradicted by a slow pour, and a stalled countdown makes the whole
  cart look broken.
- **No push notifications.** A page opened from a QR code can't notify anyone on
  iOS without being installed to the home screen first, and nobody installs an app
  for a free coffee. The tab chimes and vibrates instead.

## The 3D Decision

"3D product viewability" is easy to say and expensive to do badly. Commissioning
a model per drink fails the menu-autonomy requirement immediately — a new
seasonal drink becomes an art request.

Instead each drink carries a **model spec** that the renderer turns into geometry
at runtime:

```js
model: {
  glass: 'tall',                                  // tall | tumbler | mug | coupe
  layers: [{ color: '#7c1024', stop: 0.45 }],     // bottom-up, stop = fill fraction
  foam: true,
  ice: true,
  garnish: 'cherry',                              // none | cherry | lime | strawberry | mint
  straw: '#e8e2d6',
}
```

![The guest ordering screen — one drink filling the screen, spinnable in 3D, no price anywhere](/images/projects/on-a-vibe/guest-drink.png)

A new drink is a form entry, not an asset pipeline. There are no 3D files in the
project at all. The owner can author a drink from a photo or a description and
tune it against a live preview that renders identically to the guest's, and the
model reacts to customisation — choosing oat milk visibly lightens the cup.

## The Business Case

The commercial argument is what justified continued investment, and it isn't the
ordering flow. Every competing cart sells a cart, a bartender, and a few hours,
so bookings are won on price or on a photo. This app adds a fourth thing that
costs nothing per event: a queue that manages itself, a bartender who isn't the
bottleneck, and **a written recap of how the guests reacted**.

The recap is the deliverable. Most carts leave behind an invoice; leaving behind
*"148 drinks, busiest at 20:30, the Biscoff Latte was the favourite, 31% of
guests came back for a second"* changes the follow-up email from a request into a
report. The recap carries no revenue figures — guests never pay, so sales metrics
would be theatre.

The test was written to be falsifiable: **the app has to win or retain roughly
one booking a year to have been worth building**, because it costs nothing to
run. So the recap is the part worth measuring, and the instruction to the owner
is to ask every host whether they read it.

### The one cost cliff, quantified

Every guest phone holds a live subscription to the event queue, and the database
bills per document delivered — so cost scales with **concurrent phones × order
updates**, not with guests. For a 120-guest, 150-drink, 3-hour event:

| Guests keep the tab open for | Avg. concurrent | Reads | Free tier (50k/day) |
|---|---|---|---|
| ~15 minutes | 10 | 15,000 | Fine |
| ~45 minutes | 30 | 27,000 | Fine |
| The whole event | 120 | **81,000** | **Exceeded** |

The status screen tells guests "keep this open — it updates on its own," so the
third row is the *designed* behaviour, not a worst case. In money the overage is
pennies. The danger is the plan, not the bill: on the free tier, exceeding the
quota doesn't cost money, it stops the database working — mid-event, in front of
the host. The recommendation is therefore to move to pay-as-you-go with a low
budget alert *before* the next real event. Cheap insurance against the only
failure a paying customer would ever see.

![The printable QR sign — the one asset needed before an event, so it sits on a public route](/images/projects/on-a-vibe/qr-sign.png)

## Prioritized Next Investments

Ordered by consequence, not by effort:

1. **Move off the free plan.** Insurance against the one visible failure.
2. **Verify allergen data against real recipes.** It's currently inferred from
   ingredient text — a legal exposure, not a feature.
3. **Export the recap as a forwardable file.** It's the commercial asset, and
   right now it only prints from a browser. A file the host can forward is a file
   that reaches whoever books next year.
4. **Let a guest cancel their own order.** Small, and it removes the one moment
   the app makes someone walk up and apologise.

Everything after that is polish. The 3D drinks are already the part people
remember; more of them is not what wins the next booking.

## Learnings

**The stated requirement was a feature; the real requirement was a relationship.**
The client asked for a QR code. The thing most likely to pay for the work is the
recap that lands in the host's inbox afterwards — a surface nobody asked for,
which only became obvious once the "who pays vs. who uses" split was written down.

**Writing down what the product is *for*, in priority order, ends scope
arguments.** Three ranked jobs meant that "should this be a menu list?" had an
answer rather than an opinion.

**Being straight about the gaps is what makes a business case usable.** Naming
that the app doesn't take money, doesn't reduce headcount, and doesn't work
without the cart is what makes the parts it *does* do believable — and it's what
turns "should we keep investing?" into a question with four numbers attached
instead of a feeling.
