## Overview

Most productivity apps help you plan. drivn helps you **start**.

The insight behind drivn is simple: people already know what needs doing. The problem is deciding what to do *right now*. drivn eliminates that friction with a **Next Best Action engine** — an algorithm that ranks your tasks by urgency, due date, and recency, then surfaces exactly one thing to focus on.

## The Problem

Decision fatigue is real. Traditional to-do apps present endless lists, nested folders, and tagging systems. The overhead of maintaining the system often outweighs the benefit of using it. Users open their task manager, spend five minutes deciding what to work on — then close it without starting anything.

Spending time [analyzing how tools like Notion handle this](/blog/notion-teardown) made the root cause clear: most productivity apps are built around *capturing and organizing* work, not *starting* it. The onboarding, the feature set, the IA — all of it is optimized for the organizational layer. The moment of action is left entirely to the user.

## The Solution

drivn removes the decision entirely. One recommended task. Always ready. No folders, no tags, no system to maintain.

## Key Features

- **Next Best Action Engine** — Ranks tasks by urgency, due date, and recency. Surfaces one task at a time.
- **Habit Tracking** — Daily habits with streaks, two priority tiers (essential vs. nice-to-have), and visual progress tracking.
- **Focus Sessions** — Distraction-free timers for deep work.
- **Momentum Tracking** — Daily streaks and calendar heatmaps to visualize consistency.
- **Web Push Notifications** — Daily reminders to keep users on track.
- **Admin Dashboard** — Real-time growth metrics, product analytics via PostHog, and error monitoring via Sentry.
- **Interactive Onboarding** — Guided first-run experience to reduce drop-off.

## Metrics & What They Taught Me

**The hypothesis going in:** removing the decision of *what to do* from the task manager experience would increase task completion compared to list-based alternatives.

**v1 results — zero marketing spend:**

| Metric | Result |
|---|---|
| Users onboarded (week 1) | 10 |
| Tasks created | 41 |
| Task completion rate | 38% |
| Highest-engagement feature | Habit tracking |
| Lowest-completion feature | Focus timer |

**What 38% actually means:** It isn't a headline win, but it's a real signal for a v1 with no onboarding optimization. More useful was the pattern *within* the data — habit tracking had significantly higher engagement than task completion, and the focus timer was opened regularly but rarely completed to the end.

That pointed to two things: users responded to the accountability structure (streaks, daily habits), but the task-to-completion loop still had friction — likely in the focus session experience itself.

**What I'd test next:** Simplify the focus timer to a single-tap start with no configuration, A/B test the default session length, and measure whether users who complete a focus session in their first three days have higher 7-day retention. The hypothesis is that the timer is being abandoned mid-session, not ignored — and that reducing the setup friction would directly improve completion rates.

## Where It Is Now

Four shipped versions later, still with zero paid acquisition:

| Metric | v1 | Current |
|---|---|---|
| Daily active users | 10 (week 1) | **40** |
| Activation rate | — | **72%** |
| Day-7 retention | — | **83%** |
| Task completion rate | 38% | **62%** |

The jump from 38% to 62% completion came from acting on exactly what the v1 data pointed at: the friction was in the task-to-completion loop, not in whether users understood the product. Onboarding was rebuilt around the accountability structure that was already working (habits and streaks) rather than around the feature tour.

## Learnings

The most important product decision on drivn was a cut, not a build. The first version had categories, subtasks, project groupings, tags, and three view modes. None of it shipped. Staying anchored to the core job-to-be-done — *help the user start the highest-priority thing* — made every scope decision easier. If a feature didn't serve that moment, it wasn't v1.
