## The Physical Product Is Excellent

Let's start with what Dollarama does right, because it's genuinely impressive.

Dollarama is the most-visited retailer in Canada by store count. The product model — high-volume, fixed low price points, constant inventory rotation — creates a shopping experience that generates real loyalty. People go to Dollarama for a specific item and leave with twelve things they didn't plan on buying. The treasure-hunt dynamic is intentional and effective.

The stores are clean, well-organized, and fast. The price proposition is clear. The checkout is efficient. The physical experience, as a product, is well-designed.

Now open your phone and try to engage with Dollarama digitally.

## The Digital Layer Doesn't Exist

There's no meaningful app. There's a website with a basic store locator and a look at current flyers. There's no account system, no loyalty program, no purchase history, no ability to check inventory before you visit.

This would be forgivable for a small regional chain. Dollarama operates over 1,500 stores across Canada. It generated over $2.9 billion in revenue in its most recent fiscal year. The absence of any digital product at this scale is a strategic choice, but I don't think it's a good one anymore.

## The Problems That Scale Without a Digital Layer

**Inventory opacity.** Dollarama's seasonal and limited items sell out fast — and there's no way to know they're available without physically going to a store. This means the customer finds out the garden section is stocked in a store across town only after driving to the closer location and finding empty shelves. The lack of real-time inventory visibility is a friction point that costs Dollarama trips they would otherwise get.

**Seasonal FOMO.** Part of Dollarama's appeal is the seasonal product rotation — Christmas decorations in November, summer accessories in May, back-to-school in August. These windows are short and the items don't come back. Customers who missed something this year have no way to be notified when it returns next year. A basic notification system ("these items are back in stock at a store near you") would directly convert interest into visits.

**The price point confusion.** Dollarama built its brand as a dollar store. The name is literally "dollar" + "rama." Items now regularly cost $3, $4, and $5 — a natural response to inflation and margin pressure. But there's no communication layer to help customers understand why, or to set expectations before they're at the register. Without a digital presence to contextualize the brand evolution, the value perception erodes in a way that's hard to measure and harder to reverse.

**No loyalty data.** Dollarama knows nothing about individual customers. They know aggregate purchasing patterns by store, but they can't identify which customers are high-frequency, what categories drive retention, or what would bring a lapsed customer back. Every other major Canadian retailer has solved this with a loyalty program. Dollarama is making category and inventory decisions without the customer-level signal that a basic account system would provide.

## What I Would Build First

Not a full app. The worst outcome here is a bloated product that tries to replicate the website, adds a barcode scanner, and calls it a day.

The minimum viable digital layer for Dollarama has one job: **reduce the uncertainty of a store visit.**

**MVP feature set:**

1. **Store inventory by category** — not SKU-level (that's complex), but category-level: "Seasonal / Summer is well-stocked at your nearest location" vs. "Limited availability." Binary signal. Reduces wasted trips.

2. **Restock and seasonal alerts** — customers opt into categories they care about. When the Christmas section goes live, they get a notification. This is a direct driver of incremental store visits with minimal engineering complexity.

3. **Flyer integration with search** — the flyer already exists on the website. Surfacing it in-app with a search function ("is there a kids' storage section this week?") adds utility to content that's already being produced.

That's it for v1. Three features. Each one directly addresses a specific friction point in the existing customer journey.

## What I'd Measure

- **Notification opt-in rate** — does the alerts feature drive meaningful signup volume?
- **Visit attribution** — do notification-triggered sessions show up as incremental store visits? (measure via location-confirmed sessions within 24 hours of a push notification)
- **Inventory check-to-visit conversion** — of users who check category availability, what % visit a store within 48 hours?
- **Category-level opt-in distribution** — which seasonal categories drive the most alerts interest? This is free product research for the buying team.

## The Strategic Case

The argument for not building a digital product is usually "our customers don't expect it" or "the ROI isn't there." I'd push back on both.

The customer base that shops at Dollarama multiple times per month is exactly the customer who would engage with an inventory alert or seasonal notification. The frequency is already there. The digital layer just needs to give them a reason to install and opt in.

And the competitive pressure is real. Dollar Tree in the US has a functional app, a loyalty program, and in-store pickup. As Dollarama continues to expand into new markets, the absence of any digital CRM starts to look like a structural gap rather than a deliberate positioning choice.

The physical product is excellent. The digital layer is a missed leverage point — one that would make the physical stores more productive without requiring a single change to the in-store experience.
