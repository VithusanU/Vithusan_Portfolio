## The Unexpected PM School

Nobody told me that running a delivery operation would prepare me to build products. But looking back, almost everything I did as an Operations Supervisor at Atripco maps directly onto what good product management looks like.

## Data Before Decisions

Every week, I generated and analyzed performance reports. Not because I was told to — but because I learned early that gut instinct doesn't scale. You can't manage 12 people, 50+ daily customer interactions, and a fleet of routes on feel alone. You need numbers.

That habit — reaching for data before drawing conclusions — is the same instinct that makes a good PM. When I started building drivn, the first thing I did wasn't write code. It was ask: *what does the data actually say about how people manage tasks?*

The answer led me to the Next Best Action engine. Not to another to-do list.

## Processes Are Products

As Operations Supervisor, one of my most impactful moves was redesigning our scheduling system. The old way: manual, conflict-prone, slow. The new way: data-driven, documented, and measurable. The result was a 40% reduction in processing time and a 30% drop in scheduling conflicts.

I didn't think of it as "process improvement" at the time. Looking back, I was doing product work. I identified a pain point, designed a solution, tested it, measured the outcome, and iterated.

## The User Is Your Team (And Your Customer)

Managing a 12-person team teaches you something that no product course will: **people don't behave the way you expect them to**. You design an onboarding doc, and half the team reads it differently. You implement a new process, and two people find a workaround on day one.

That's not failure — that's user research. The best product teams build feedback loops. The best operations supervisors do too.

## Bringing It Together

When I transitioned into software development and started pursuing product management, I didn't leave operations behind. I brought it with me. The metrics mindset. The process thinking. The habit of starting with the user's actual problem, not the solution I'd already imagined.

That's the frame I build from now. And I think it's what makes product people who've operated in the real world a little different from those who haven't.
