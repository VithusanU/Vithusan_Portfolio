## The Problem I Kept Ignoring

I had a to-do list problem. Not the kind where you don't have a system — I had too many systems. Notion, Apple Reminders, sticky notes. The lists were long, organized, and completely useless by Wednesday.

The real problem wasn't organization. It was **starting**. I'd open my task manager, spend five minutes deciding what to work on, feel overwhelmed, and close it.

I started asking: what if the app just *told me* what to do next?

## The Idea: One Task, Always Ready

Most productivity apps solve for *capture* and *organization*. drivn solves for *initiation*. The core idea is the **Next Best Action** — a single, algorithmically selected task that surfaces the moment you open the app.

No list to sort. No decision to make. Just: *do this*.

![drivn home screen — one task, always ready](/images/gallery/drivn-dashboard.jpg)

## Designing the Ranking Algorithm

The NBA engine ranks tasks by combining three signals:

1. **Urgency** — Is it overdue? Due today? Due this week?
2. **Recency** — Has this task been sitting untouched for a long time?
3. **Priority** — Did the user mark it as important?

The weights aren't fixed. I tuned them through personal use over several weeks, watching which tasks the algorithm surfaced and whether they felt right. When a task surfaced at the top and felt wrong, I'd trace back to why and adjust the weighting.

This is iterative product design applied to an algorithm. Same principle, different medium.

## Scope Creep Almost Killed It

The first version had: task categories, subtasks, project groupings, tags, collaborative sharing, and three view modes.

None of it shipped. I was building a project management tool when I wanted to build a focus tool. The product brief I wrote before building anything said: *"Help users start the most important thing, right now."* The feature list contradicted that brief entirely.

I cut it down to the core: tasks, habits, focus sessions, and the NBA engine. That's what shipped.

## What I Learned From Real Users

![Habit tracker — the highest-engagement feature in drivn v1](/images/gallery/drivn-habits.jpg)

After launch, the admin dashboard told a story. 10 users signed up. 41 tasks created. 25 completed. **38% task completion rate**.

38% sounds low — but for a v1 with no onboarding optimization and no marketing, it's a real signal. The habit tracker had the highest engagement of any feature. The focus timer was used but rarely completed.

That's a backlog insight: habit tracking is sticky, focus sessions need work. In a real PM role, that's the next sprint.

## Tech Choices as Product Decisions

Every technical decision in drivn was a product decision:

- **Supabase over Firebase** — Better SQL structure for task relationships, cleaner pricing at scale
- **Zustand over Redux** — Less boilerplate meant faster iteration on state logic
- **PostHog for analytics** — Event-based tracking so I could measure exactly what I cared about
- **PWA over native app** — Zero install friction for early users

![Admin dashboard — real user metrics after launch](/images/gallery/drivn-admin.jpg)

## What's Next

drivn is live and being used. The next iteration focuses on improving the onboarding flow (current drop-off is visible in PostHog), refining the completion tracking UX, and exploring a Spotify integration for focus sessions.

Building drivn was the most complete product experience I've had — from idea to shipped product to real user data. That's the loop I want to run, at scale, in a PM role.
