## The Promise

Notion sells itself as the all-in-one workspace — notes, wikis, databases, project management, docs. The product is genuinely impressive. The flexibility is real. The community of power users who build elaborate second-brain systems in it is vocal and passionate.

So why do so many people install it, poke around for a week, and quietly go back to Apple Notes?

## The Real Problem Isn't Features

Notion's problem isn't what it can't do. It's what it asks of you before it gives you anything back.

Open Notion for the first time and you're dropped into a blank sidebar with a few example pages. There's no question asking what you're trying to accomplish. No guided setup. No "start here." Just a canvas that can become anything — which is immediately paralyzing for someone who doesn't already know what they want it to become.

This is a classic **activation gap**. The user signed up because they want to be more organized. Notion can absolutely deliver that. But the path from "signed up" to "got value" requires a level of upfront design work that most people won't do.

The product assumes the user already knows how to use Notion. The onboarding teaches you what Notion *is*, not how to *solve your problem with it*.

## What App Store Reviews Actually Say

Filter Notion's App Store reviews to 1–3 stars and a pattern appears immediately:

- *"Too complicated, I don't know where to start"*
- *"Beautiful app but I have no idea how to set it up"*
- *"Gave up after an hour trying to figure out the templates"*
- *"I just want to take notes. Why is this so complicated?"*

These aren't complaints about missing features. They're complaints about the **time-to-value problem**. The user's job-to-be-done in the first session is: *feel like I got something done*. Notion's onboarding doesn't prioritize that job.

## The Power User Cliff

Notion's retention curve almost certainly looks bimodal. There's a group that churns in the first two weeks, and a group that becomes deeply hooked and builds increasingly elaborate setups. There's very little middle ground.

The power users are vocal. They post their setups on YouTube and Reddit. They make Notion look aspirational. But they also create a false impression of what the typical user experience looks like — because typical users never get close to that level.

The product is optimized for the engaged minority in a way that creates barriers for the silent majority.

## The Template Problem

Notion does offer templates. There are hundreds of them in the gallery. And they're part of the problem.

The templates are organized by type — Personal, Work, Engineering, Design — but they're not organized by the user's situation. A new user doesn't know if they want a "second brain" template or a "weekly planner" template or a "meeting notes" template. They just know they want to feel less scattered.

Presenting 200 templates to someone who can't articulate their organizational style yet is not helpful. It's another version of the blank canvas problem with better-looking options.

## What I'd Change

**A short setup survey before anything else.** Three questions, max:

1. *What's the main thing you want to use Notion for?* (Notes, Projects, Team wiki, All of the above)
2. *Are you moving from another tool?* (Yes — which one? / No, starting fresh)
3. *Are you using this solo or with a team?*

Based on those answers, Notion pre-populates a workspace that's relevant to the user's actual situation. Not a template they have to configure — a workspace that's already partially set up and waiting for content.

The goal is to get the user to their first real "I made something useful" moment in under five minutes.

**Fewer templates, better positioned.** Instead of 200 community templates, surface three "Starter Paths" based on the most common use cases. Each path is opinionated. It makes decisions for the user so they don't have to.

**Progressive disclosure on power features.** Databases, linked views, and relational properties are what make Notion special. But they're also what make it feel overwhelming. Hide them until the user has had a few successful sessions with simpler blocks. Surface them with in-context prompts: *"Your table is getting complex — want to turn it into a database?"*

## What I'd Measure

- **7-day activation rate** — % of new users who create at least one page with meaningful content (not just the default template)
- **Time to first non-template page** — how long it takes a new user to create something original
- **Setup survey completion rate** — does the guided setup increase 7-day retention?
- **Template adoption vs. abandonment** — which templates get used vs. opened and immediately closed

The core hypothesis: removing the blank canvas from the first session would increase 7-day activation rate by a meaningful margin. I'd run that as an A/B test against the current default-empty experience.

## The Underlying Tension

What makes this hard for Notion is that their positioning — the all-in-one, make-it-yours workspace — is fundamentally at odds with opinionated onboarding. The more you guide users toward a specific setup, the more you're asserting what Notion is for. That conflicts with the "it can be anything" brand promise.

But the data, I suspect, would show that guided opinionated onboarding converts more users than infinite flexibility on day one. You can always let them break out of the rails later. The job in the first session is just to give them something worth coming back to.
